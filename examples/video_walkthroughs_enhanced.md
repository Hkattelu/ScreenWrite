## Content

### Section 1: Guide Books and Hotlines

[Show: cover of "Mastering Pac-Man" book]
The earliest instances of walkthroughs I could find were two books from 1981: "Mastering Pac-Man" and "How to Master the Video Games."

[Annotation: "1981 - First video game guidebook"]
From my research, "Mastering Pac-Man" was the first bookâ€”and also the first video-game-specific book.

[Display: pages from Mastering Pac-Man showing diagrams and tables]
It was a relatively text-heavy book, but it had a handful of visuals. Ken Uston, the author, also included visuals of the specific patterns he would use to get high scores. He included tables and diagrams of the enemies and items. And he even had chapters on follow-up games like Ms. Pac-Man.

[B-roll: person flipping through vintage game guide]
The book is organized nicely into chapters, and it's genuinely cool to read and learn about. One thing that really impressed me is that he even included a section on how the later manufacturers changed the chips, which broke some of the old patterns that gamers used.

[Show: cover of "How to Beat the Video Games" by Michael Blanchet]
The next interesting book that I could find a PDF of came a year afterward in 1982: "How to Beat the Video Games" by Michael Blanchet. This book and a few of the ones that followed were more like anthologies.

[Annotation: "Games were SHORT - one book covered many games"]
Games weren't super long, so the books would walk you through how to play a lot of different games like Donkey Kong, Galaga, or Asteroids.

[Visual: old arcade games from the early 80s]
Let's take a closer look at "How to Beat the Video Games." The first walkthroughs really touched upon basics that we take for granted today.

[Screenshot: basics chapter from the guide]
It starts with the gamer basics: like how to put coins into the machine without them getting stuck. No need to punch or kick the machines!

[Show: static shock prevention tips section]
It talks about how to avoid static shock at the early arcade room floors by grounding yourself with a four-step sequence.

[Display: controls and efficiency section]
To how to best operate the buttons and joysticks in the most efficient ways possible. Little things I've never seen in a guidebook today.

[B-roll: vintage arcade cabinet with player using controls]
Even though this is a book that was published to sell, I think it's cool to see the love and passion that the author, Michael Blanchet, put into it. There was also a lot of room for creativity here in these guides with the use of diagrams, the writing, and the organization.

[Show: visuals from the game E.T.]
In 1983, the video game industry crashed, and we didn't see a whole lot of innovation in the walkthrough space for a while. But the next new innovation came with video game help phone hotlines.

[Display: Nintendo Power hotline advertisement]
The best example of these I could find was the Nintendo Power Hotline that ran from 1987 to 2005, and briefly returned in 2016.

[Annotation: "1987-2005 - Nintendo Power Hotline era"]
It's a really weird concept, but basically, you could call a phone number and someone would be there to help answer your questions about what to do or how to progress. You could actually send physical mail too, since the phone tolls could get expensive.

[Show: interview clips discussing the hotline experience]
I did find an interview with an old Nintendo gameplay counselor by the AV Club. They discuss what it was like to answer gaming questions all day.

[B-roll: stock footage of a tutor teaching a student]
While the hotline was definitely not the best type of walkthrough, it was cool in that you could get real-time feedback. For example, if you didn't understand something the way it was explained in the book, the counselor could walk you through it like a personal tutor for you.

[Display: another Nintendo Power hotline advertisement]
It was also really up-to-date information, because the counselors had to be playing the games too.

### Section 2: The Rise of Internet-Based Text Walkthroughs

[Annotation: "1990s - The internet changes everything"]
When I was growing up, my favorite walkthroughs to use were text-based internet-hosted walkthroughs, like the ones you'd find on Cheatcodes.com, GameFAQs, or CheatCodeCentral.

[Screenshot: GameFAQs.com showing multiple game guides]
One thing that's great about these is that they're all still up, and you can just go through and see how many of them there are.

[Show: Final Fantasy Tactics A2 guide by Warfreak]
The best one I remember using is the Final Fantasy Tactics A2 guide by Warfreak.

[Display: table of contents of the Tactics A2 guide]
The basic format of these is that you'd have a table of contents and a typed-up walkthrough of the game from start to finish.

[Screenshot: classes and jobs information section]
You'd usually see some information sections and sections about specific collectibles or achievements too at the start or the end.

[Show: version history and update notes]
Because they were on the internet, the authors would also update them every once in a while and include notes on what they changed.

[ScreenWrite: screen recording of using Ctrl+F to search the guide]
What was really useful about the guides is that you could use Ctrl+F in your browser to find any specific text you needed and get right to the heart of the issue you were having. I also found that these guides tended to be really concise and detailed, which was useful. There wasn't any fluff.

[Screenshot: Jak & Daxter collectible section with text descriptions]
That being said, because they were text-heavy, it would sometimes be pretty hard to visualize what the guide was saying. This was really annoying for things like finding collectibles, where a lot of the times the guides would be really bad at describing the directions.

[Show: ASCII-based maps from guides]
Some writers would try to draw ASCII-based maps, but I found those pretty confusing.

[Annotation: "Key insight: Text walkthroughs were UNMONETIZED and COMMUNITY-DRIVEN"]
It's important to note that text-based walkthroughs were the only truly unmonetized guide. The writers didn't get paid by Nintendo or make money off book sales. They also helped create a sense of community within games.

[B-roll: people browsing forums and gaming communities]
Forums were also popular at this time, and most games often had multiple different people writing guides for them. It felt like knowledge and discovery of the game was shared amongst the player base.

[Display: official game guidebook for Final Fantasy 12]
Speaking of monetization, the other type of guide that got popular in this era were official game-specific guidebooks. These were different from the books of the past in that they were supported by game developers. This meant they had way more detail that individual gamers wouldn't know aboutâ€”like specific stat numbers on abilities or extremely obscure game mechanics.

[Show: pages from guidebook with maps and artwork]
They also included way more visuals, art, and personality than the books from the 80s. I still remember reading the Final Fantasy 12 guidebook as a kid. It was genuinely super fun to look through the maps and learn about the world with a physical book.

[Annotation: "Official guides were expensive but high quality"]
The main problem with these is just that they were kind of expensive, and because you had to get one for every game, the price would seriously start to rack up.

### Section 3: Modern Guidesâ€”IGN, Wikis, and Videos

[Annotation: "2010s-2020s - The age of infinite content"]
Fast-forward to today. Gamers rely mostly on three new types of guides which didn't really exist before.

[Show: IGN guide page for a popular game]
The first one that's the most Google-search-optimized is the IGN-style guide. IGN hosts walkthrough pages for a lot of games. They're kind of similar to the text-based walkthrough pages, but they'll usually include visuals as well.

[B-roll: clicking through multiple IGN guide pages]
While the individual pages are usually good quality, a huge pain I have with these guides is just how much they make you click around to different pages to navigate. Now I get why they do thisâ€”it's to monetize the site by trying to get you to see more ads on page loads.

[Annotation: "Quality compromised by monetization model"]
I know IGN has to pay the bills, but it sucks that the quality of these guides is seriously compromised by how hard they are to navigate.

[Show: YouTube walkthrough video thumbnails]
Another form that's hard to navigate is YouTube video walkthroughs. Usually, the video walkthroughs will come either as long-form playthroughs or as three- to seven-minute videos walking you through specific encounters or builds.

[Display: montage of different YouTube guide quality levels]
The quality of YouTube walkthroughs varies WILDLY. There are creators who do an amazing job of keeping things concise and using visuals to help you understand. But at the same time, there are creators that just drag out things that could be explained in 30 seconds to ten-minute videos so they can optimize for ad revenue.

[B-roll: someone recording their gameplay footage]
The reason these guides really blew up is that it is simply way easier to just record yourself playing a game and walking through it than it is to manually write up a detailed, high-quality guide. You can also monetize on YouTube, which is a big appeal.

[Annotation: "YouTube lowered the barrier to entry significantly"]
You don't even have to be very good at editing videos to make a good guideâ€”you just have to be concise and clear with your speech and visuals.

[Show: wiki walkthrough for Elden Ring]
The last thing I want to mention is walkthroughs on Wikis today. The best example I can think of is the Fextralife Wiki walkthrough for Elden Ring. These wiki pages are sort of like IGN walkthroughs, but they're more well-organized and community-driven.

[Display: wiki page with maps, links, and inline images]
They include tons of links and pointers to specific gear, maps, or bosses. They even include inline images and pointers to YouTube videos.

[B-roll: browsing through comprehensive wiki guide]
If I'm being honest, I can't really think of a downside to them. That being said, I've never actually used them, nor do I know anyone who's used them. But they seem really useful and high quality. Let me know in the comments what you think about them or if there are any downsides.

### Section 4: The Old Format in the Modern Era

[Annotation: "Why do I prefer old-school text walkthroughs?"]
Now that we've gone through all the walkthrough formats, I wanted to bring it back to text-based walkthroughs. Today, they feel like such a lost art.

[Show: peaceful scene of reading text document]
In an age of forced ad breaks and pointless slop to fill time, they feel like a breath of fresh air. No ads, no monetizationâ€”just concise information communicated in an efficient format that's super easy to search.

[B-roll: downloading and opening text file]
You can download it and take it wherever you want, and it'll barely take up any memory.

[Visual: Chrono Trigger game footage]
The next game I'm planning on playing is Chrono Trigger, and I'm going to go out of my way to use a text-based walkthrough to get that late-90s experience.

[Annotation: "But am I just being nostalgic?"]
But the truth is, I'm romanticizing the past. Text-based guides never had the visuals that wikis do today. They were concise, but it's really hard to be clear without visuals. Plus, no one ever got paid for any of the hard work they did to put together the walkthroughs.

[Show: modern wiki with images, maps, and links]
I feel like wikis with walkthroughs have the best of all worlds: community, clarity, and detail.

[Annotation: "Text walkthroughs might never be the dominant format again"]
At the end of the day, text walkthroughs might never be the dominant format again. But for some of us, they'll always be the gold standard for quickly getting the answers we need.
